What’s in your stress resilience toolkit?

It’s hard to find time or prioritise self-care when there are million things going on and each staking a huge claim on your attention and energy. Many individuals, especially women, find it hard to carve time out for themselves as they feel guilty for prioritising their own needs above everyone else’s. So, before stress engulfs your mind and body, it’s important to have stress resilience plan in which self-care is high on priority.

But before we make a stress resilience toolkit, let’s understand what stress is and the different ways it can show up and affect our mind, body, emotions and behaviour.

 

What is stress?
Stress is usually described as a situation where we perceive that we don’t have control over the situation or circumstance. There is also a physiological response that involves the nervous system.

 

Are there different types of stress?

There are two types of stress –

  1. Acute –Acute stress is experienced when something upsetting or unexpected happens to us. It can last from a few weeks to several months and can be very intense. For example, a sudden loss of a loved one, a natural disaster, sudden diagnosis of a health condition, or assault.

 

  1. Chronic – Chronic stress experienced when we have been under pressure for a long time. For example, going through a long and arduous divorce, losing a job and experiencing financial difficulties, facing impossible deadlines, caring for a chronically ill or aging family member.

 

How can stress affect you?

Stress can have a direct and indirect impact on our mental, emotional, physical and behavioural health, including:

Mental and emotional:

  • Feeling tired
  • Exhaustion from carrying everyone else’s emotional load.
  • Sad, anxious or afraid
  • Tearful or emotional
  • Slow decision making and mental fatigue
  • Reduced focus, concentration and productivity, taking longer to complete tasks
  • Feeling irritated and snapping at your partner or kids

 

Physical:

  • High blood pressure or heart palpitations
  • Headaches, clenched jaw, hunched shoulders, muscle pain, joint pain
  • Menopausal hormonal changes leaving you with low mood, irritability, hot flushes, insomnia, brain fog and fatigue
  • Difficulty sleeping or waking up exhausted despite long hours of sleep
  • Digestive and gut issues like IBS, constipation or acid reflux
  • Frequent infections, feeling run-down, sore or inflamed.
  • Mid-afternoon energy slumps
  • Lower libido

Behavioural:

  • Impulsive spending
  • Social isolation or getting into arguments or fights.
  • Lack of interest in things, a general lack of joy or cynicism
  • Doubting self, feeling overwhelmed and trapped
  • Reliance on foods or alcohol to cope
  • Being indecisive or inflexible
  • Binging on Netflix, YouTube, sweets and snacks

 

How can an integrated Health, Wellness and Resilience coach help?

When the nervous system has been operating in the ‘fight or flight’ mode for long, it needs to be brought back into a state of balance and calm through ‘rest and digest’ activities.

Often, individuals know that they aren’t feeling their usual self, but don’t know where to start, or give themselves the time, space and permission to take care of themselves and create more space in their mind and body to deal with stress in a healthy way before it starts becoming burnout.

As an integrated Health, Wellness and Resilience Coach, there are three foundational questions I ask my clients:

  1. How does stress show up for you?
  2. What are you currently doing to unwind and relax?
  3. What can you do differently?

The response to question #2 will usually be around some unhealthy choices like the use of food, cigarettes, over-exercising, using alcohol or withdrawing further. There are several other options to unwind and relax and complete the stress cycle instead of gravitating towards unhealthy options. Let’s explore some options.

 

What can I include in my stress resilience toolkit?

While there are many ways to close the stress cycle and return to the ‘rest and digest’ state of being, here are 5 healthy and balanced lifestyle habits that you can start exploring and make your own:

  • Journaling

Grab a piece of paper or a notebook and start writing. Don’t censor yourself, just dump all your thoughts and to-do lists on a sheet of paper. The act of taking pen to paper is not only cathartic but also reduces screen time and helps to improve focus. You don’t have to show your journal to anyone, so write away!

You may prefer to respond to a set of questions, note your thoughts in bullet points or write in long-form. Whatever method you choose, make this a daily practise. Some of the benefits of journaling are that it helps us release the grip of the mind, creates more space, bring things to conscious, allows us to see patterns and make connections, find solutions rather than stay with the problem.

 

  • Make mindfulness, meditation or some spiritual practice a part of your every day

I’ve been meditating since my second year of university. Since then, my experience with meditation has taken on many hues – from Jappa meditation to Pranyama and Breathwork to Mindfulness to guided meditations using an app. Regardless, Mindfulness and Meditation are non-negotiable parts of my early morning routine. They help me start the day grounded, calmer, and more aligned with my energy before the day and people start placing their demands on my energy. Sometimes, I listen to a couple of meditations while finishing my morning walk.

If you are new to mindfulness or meditation, you can start with free guided mediations on an app or even YouTube. Even just focussing on your breath, counting your inhales and exhales can be a great way to ease yourself into the practise of mindfulness.

 

  • Get moving or exercising

Don’t like exercise, then incorporate some form of movement in your daily schedule. Even a 20-30-minute walk in nature or a short Yoga or Pilates session will do wonders for your mental and physical wellbeing by lowering your stress hormone Cortisol and Adrenaline and releases the ‘feel-good’ hormones and neurotransmitters – Dopamine (the ‘reward chemical’ that drives motivation, learning and pleasure), Serotonin (the ‘mood stabiliser’ that helps regulate your overall sense of well-being, emotions, sleep and appetite) and Endorphins (the body’s natural pain killer and mood booster). If you like more active forms of movement, then running, jogging, lighting weights, swimming, cycling can be other options. A good balance of movement/exercise includes cardio, strength and resistance training.

Movement also burns off excess nervous energy, and relieves any built up physical tension. Focusing on the breath while moving acts as a meditation with the benefit of breaking the loop of cyclical or racing thoughts.

 

  • Focus on quality sleep

Sleep is essential for our overall health and wellbeing as it allows our body and brain to repair, recover and reset. When we sleep, our memories are consolidated, toxins are cleared out, hormones are regulated helping control hunger and satiety levels, physical healing occurs through the release of growth hormones.

When we aren’t able to have a set sleep routine and good quality sleep, we are more vulnerable to mood swings, changes in appetite, make unhealthy food choices, have low reaction time, aren’t able to concentrate, feel more pressure and overwhelm and all of this affects our work and our relationships.

Everyone’s sleep requirement is different. Sometimes 8-9 hours of sleep can still leave us feeling tired, so focus on quality of sleep more than the number of hours.

Things you can do to get good quality sleep:

  • Have a sleep routine – go to bed and wake up at the same time
  • Avoid blue light emitting devices 2-3 hours before going to bed.
  • Use white noise or some calming music to help you relax and unwind
  • Sip on some warm herbal teas or turmeric milk before going to bed
  • Keep the temperature of the bedroom approximately between temperature is around 16-18°C (60-65°F).
  • Journal a few minutes before you go to sleep, getting thoughts and to-do lists that might keep you awake out of your head on onto paper.

 

  • Eating balanced and nutrition meals

East foods that help lower the stress hormone Cortisol, fight inflammation and boost feel-good neurotransmitters like Serotonin.

 

Incorporate inflammation reducing sourced like: fatty fish (like salmon) or tuna, magnesium-rich leafy greens like spinach, magnesium-rich nuts like pumpkin seeds, walnuts, chia seeds, fermented foods like kombucha, kefir or kimchi that nourish the gut-brain axis, vitamin C rich foods like strawberries, oranges that lower Cortisol and support immunity during demanding times.

Eat a well-timed and balanced meal to help lower your stress. Keeping a gap of 2-3 hours between the last meal of the day before going to bed will help prevent indigestion and let the body siphon energy to essential repair works.

 

The bottom line

These are just some ideas that you can start exploring, making your own and adding to your stress resilience toolkit. Start small and slowly but surely incorporate what works for you. Pick one thing, try it regularly and then try another. The last thing you want to do when you are already stressed and overwhelmed is to add more to your to-do list.

When your mind, body, and nervous system are under constant strain, healthy and balanced living stops being sustainable. So, as the saying goes – “A stich in time, saves nine’. Make your own stress resilience tool kit. Don’t wait until you are stressed. Practice your go-to tools and habits all year round to gain maximum benefit.
